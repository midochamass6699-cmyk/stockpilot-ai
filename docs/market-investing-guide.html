Understand what you are investing in before you choose it

Stocks, penny stocks and cryptocurrencies are often discussed together, but they represent different types of assets. They have different market structures, sources of value, trading characteristics and risks.

This guide is designed for people who want to understand the stock market, explore penny stocks, learn about digital assets and develop a more disciplined approach to investment research. It explains key concepts in plain language and offers a framework for comparing opportunities instead of relying on hype, tips or a low share price alone.

Important: There is no universally “best” stock, penny stock or cryptocurrency. A suitable investment depends on an individual's goals, time horizon, financial situation and ability to tolerate losses. Research can help you understand an asset; it cannot guarantee a return.
Section 1

How financial markets work

A financial market is a place or system where buyers and sellers trade assets. In the stock market, investors buy and sell shares of publicly traded companies. In cryptocurrency markets, participants trade digital assets on crypto exchanges and other trading platforms.

Prices change as buyers and sellers respond to company results, economic data, interest rates, industry developments, regulation, investor expectations and news. A price is not simply a measure of what an asset is worth; it is the current level at which market participants are willing to transact.

Stocks

Shares represent an ownership interest in a company. Investors may benefit if the business grows and the market values its shares more highly.

Penny stocks

A broad informal label for lower-priced shares, often associated with smaller companies and greater trading or disclosure risks.

Cryptocurrency

Digital assets that use cryptographic systems. Some networks use blockchains to record transactions and maintain shared records.

Primary markets and secondary markets

In a primary offering, a company or issuer sells securities to raise capital. In the secondary market, investors trade existing securities with one another. Most everyday stock trading takes place in secondary markets through exchanges and brokerage platforms.

Exchanges, brokers and trading platforms

A stock exchange provides a marketplace and listing framework for securities. A brokerage firm gives customers access to markets and may provide research, order tools and account services. A crypto exchange or trading platform facilitates the buying and selling of digital assets, subject to its own products, rules and jurisdiction.

Before opening an account, review fees, available markets, order types, custody arrangements, account protections, withdrawal rules and the regulatory status of the provider where you live.

Section 2

Traditional stocks: what they are and how to evaluate them

A stock, also called a share or equity, represents a fractional ownership interest in a corporation. When you buy a publicly traded stock, you become a shareholder. Depending on the share class, you may have voting rights and may receive dividends if the company's board declares them.

Common categories of stocks

  • Large-cap stocks: Shares of companies with relatively large market capitalizations. Size does not eliminate investment risk.
  • Mid-cap stocks: Companies between commonly used small-cap and large-cap ranges. Definitions vary by index provider and market.
  • Small-cap stocks: Shares of smaller public companies. They may have greater growth potential, but can also have less liquidity and higher volatility.
  • Growth stocks: Companies investors expect to grow revenue or earnings faster than the broader market. Their valuations can be sensitive to changing expectations.
  • Value stocks: Shares that appear inexpensive relative to selected measures of business value. A low valuation can also reflect real business problems.
  • Dividend stocks: Companies that distribute some earnings or cash to shareholders. Dividends are not guaranteed and can be reduced or suspended.
  • Technology, energy, financial and healthcare stocks: Sector groupings that help investors compare companies operating in related industries.

What to examine before buying a stock

Start with the business, not just the ticker symbol. Understand what the company sells, who its customers are, how it earns money, what could affect demand and who its competitors are. Then review its financial statements and valuation.

Research areaQuestions to ask
RevenueAre sales growing, stable or declining? Is growth coming from the core business?
ProfitabilityDoes the company generate operating profit and sustainable earnings?
Cash flowDoes the business generate cash, or does it regularly need external financing?
DebtCan the company reasonably service its debt under different economic conditions?
ValuationHow does the share price compare with earnings, sales, cash flow, assets and peers?
ManagementIs leadership experienced, transparent and aligned with shareholders?
CompetitionDoes the company have a defensible advantage, or can competitors easily take its customers?

What is market capitalization?

Market capitalization, often called market cap, is calculated by multiplying a company's share price by its number of outstanding shares. It is one way to describe the market value of a company's equity. It is not the same as the company's enterprise value, total assets or cash available to shareholders.

For example, a company with 100 million shares outstanding and a share price of $10 has a market capitalization of $1 billion. A stock's low share price does not automatically mean the company is cheap: the number of shares outstanding and the company's financial condition matter.

Section 3

Penny stocks: meaning, opportunities and risks

Penny stocks are generally understood to be shares trading at a very low price, often issued by smaller or less-established companies. The exact definition varies by country, regulator, exchange and market participant. In the United States, the term is often associated with shares priced below US$5, but not every low-priced stock has the same regulatory or trading characteristics. Canadian usage can differ.

Some penny stocks trade on established exchanges, while others trade over the counter (OTC). A low share price alone does not tell you whether a company is financially sound, fairly valued or easy to trade.

Why do investors search for penny stocks?

  • They may be interested in smaller companies and emerging industries.
  • They may look for businesses with a new product, contract, discovery or other potential catalyst.
  • They may want to research companies that receive less mainstream analyst coverage.
  • They may be attracted by the possibility of large percentage price movements.

Why penny stocks can be especially risky

  • Low liquidity: There may be few buyers or sellers, making it difficult to enter or exit at a desired price.
  • Wide bid-ask spreads: The difference between the highest bid and lowest ask can be substantial.
  • Volatility: Prices can move sharply on limited trading activity or news.
  • Limited information: Some issuers provide less information or have shorter operating histories.
  • Dilution: A company may issue additional shares, reducing existing shareholders' percentage ownership.
  • Financial distress: Some low-priced companies face weak cash flow, heavy debt or a risk of insolvency.
  • Promotion and manipulation: Aggressive online promotion, misleading claims and coordinated trading can create artificial demand.
Do not confuse a low share price with a bargain. A $0.10 stock is not necessarily cheaper in valuation terms than a $100 stock. Examine market capitalization, share count, debt, cash, business quality and the risks of trading the security.

A penny stock research checklist

  1. Confirm the company's legal name, ticker, exchange and jurisdiction.
  2. Find the latest financial statements and material company filings.
  3. Review cash, debt, operating cash flow and the company's need for additional financing.
  4. Check the number of shares outstanding and any warrants, options or convertible securities.
  5. Look at average trading volume, bid-ask spread and recent price history.
  6. Identify the specific business development that could change the company's prospects, and verify it through primary sources.
  7. Search for trading halts, regulatory notices, going-concern warnings and other material risks.
  8. Decide in advance what evidence would invalidate your investment thesis.

How to research Canadian and US penny stocks

Canadian investors may encounter small issuers listed on the TSX Venture Exchange, the Canadian Securities Exchange and other venues. US investors may encounter small-cap shares on Nasdaq or the NYSE as well as OTC securities. Listing standards, reporting obligations, trading rules and investor protections can differ substantially. Check the relevant exchange and regulator rather than assuming all penny stocks are alike.

Useful primary research sources include company filings, exchange notices and regulator databases. In Canada, look for SEDAR+ issuer filings and relevant Canadian securities-regulator information. In the United States, review SEC company filings, including EDGAR, and relevant FINRA investor resources.

Section 4

Cryptocurrency: Bitcoin, Ethereum and digital assets

Cryptocurrency is a category of digital asset. Many cryptocurrencies operate on blockchain networks: distributed systems that maintain a record of transactions using cryptographic techniques and network rules. Not every digital asset works in the same way, and the word “crypto” covers a wide range of projects and designs.

Common types of crypto assets

  • Bitcoin (BTC): A decentralized digital asset with a capped issuance schedule defined by its protocol. Its price can be highly volatile.
  • Ethereum (ETH): The native asset of the Ethereum network, which supports smart contracts and decentralized applications.
  • Altcoins: A broad informal term for cryptocurrencies other than Bitcoin. Projects vary widely in purpose, design and risk.
  • Stablecoins: Digital tokens designed to maintain a relatively stable value, often in relation to a fiat currency. Their reserves, redemption terms and issuer risks require careful review.
  • Utility or governance tokens: Tokens that may be used within a network or provide certain participation rights. Their actual functions and legal treatment vary.

How to evaluate a cryptocurrency

Do not rely only on token price, social media attention or a ranking by market capitalization. Investigate the purpose of the project, whether the technology works, how the network is used, how tokens are distributed and what risks could affect adoption.

Crypto research factorWhat to investigate
Use caseWhat problem does the network or token aim to solve? Is there real demand?
AdoptionAre people and developers using the network? Are activity measures meaningful and independently verifiable?

Crypto market capitalization and trading volume

Crypto market capitalization is commonly calculated as token price multiplied by circulating supply. It is a useful reference measure, but it does not mean that the same amount of cash could be withdrawn at that price. Trading volume estimates how much of an asset changed hands over a period; reported volume quality can vary by venue and data provider.

Wallets, custody and security

Crypto ownership and access depend on the asset and custody arrangement. A custodial platform may hold assets on a customer's behalf. A self-custody wallet gives the user control of private keys, which also means the user is responsible for protecting recovery phrases and signing transactions correctly. Transfers can be irreversible. Never share a seed phrase or private key.

Section 6

How to find and evaluate investment opportunities

A disciplined research process helps separate evidence from excitement. Whether you are looking for stocks to watch today, undervalued stocks, high-volume penny stocks or cryptocurrencies with growing adoption, use a repeatable method.

Step 1: Define what you are looking for

Decide whether you are researching a long-term business investment, a short-term trading setup, a dividend strategy, a small-cap growth company or a digital asset. These are different objectives and require different evidence. Set your time horizon before comparing candidates.

Step 2: Build a watchlist using transparent filters

Screeners can help organize a large market into a manageable list. Possible filters include market capitalization, exchange, share price, average daily volume, revenue growth, profitability, debt, valuation, sector and recent news. For crypto, filters may include market capitalization, liquidity, circulating supply, network activity and exchange availability.

Filters are discovery tools, not buy signals. A stock that passes a screen still needs individual research, and a high-volume asset is not automatically a good investment.

Step 3: Read primary sources

Start with the company's filings, earnings releases, investor presentations and official exchange notices. Compare company claims with audited financial statements and regulatory disclosures. For crypto, read the project's technical documentation, token information, governance proposals, security reports and verifiable network data.

Step 4: Examine fundamentals

For companies, review revenue, margins, earnings, cash flow, debt, share dilution, competitive position and management. For tokens, examine the use case, token economics, supply schedule, adoption, developer activity, security and governance. Ask what could make the asset more or less valuable over time.

Step 5: Consider valuation and expectations

A strong business can still be a poor investment if its share price already assumes unrealistic growth. Compare valuation ratios with relevant peers and the company's history, while remembering that accounting measures have limitations. For crypto, traditional valuation ratios may not apply; be cautious with models that claim to produce a precise fair value for a token.

Step 6: Check liquidity and trading conditions

Review average trading volume, bid-ask spread, order-book depth and the market venue. Thinly traded assets can move sharply when a larger order arrives. Consider whether you could exit a position without causing significant price impact.

Step 7: Identify catalysts and counterarguments

Potential catalysts include earnings reports, product launches, regulatory decisions, new contracts, industry changes or network upgrades. Write down both the positive case and the negative case. A balanced analysis asks what evidence would prove the original thesis wrong.

Step 8: Make a plan before acting

Define your intended holding period, position size, risk limit and the conditions that would make you reconsider. Avoid making decisions solely because a stock is trending, a token is rising quickly or an online post promises a rapid gain.

Section 7

How to read stock charts and technical indicators

Technical analysis studies price and trading activity. Traders use charts to describe trends, volatility, momentum and possible areas of supply or demand. Technical indicators summarize past market data; they do not predict the future with certainty.

Basic chart concepts

  • Candlestick chart: Shows opening, high, low and closing prices for a selected time period.
  • Trend: A broad direction of price movement, often described as upward, downward or sideways.
  • Support and resistance: Price areas where buying or selling interest has previously appeared. These are zones, not guaranteed barriers.
  • Trading volume: The number of shares or units traded during a period. Volume can help put a price move in context.
  • Moving average: A rolling average of price over a chosen number of periods, sometimes used to describe trend direction.
  • Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator. Common reference levels such as 70 and 30 are not automatic sell or buy signals.
  • MACD: A trend and momentum indicator based on moving averages.

Technical analysis is often combined with fundamental research and risk controls. A chart pattern cannot tell you whether a company is solvent, whether a token is secure or whether a news claim is true.

Section 8

Risk management: protect your decision-making

All investing involves risk, including the possible loss of some or all of the money invested. Higher potential returns generally come with uncertainty, and assets with thin liquidity or limited disclosure can be particularly difficult to evaluate.

Practical risk-management principles

  • Diversification: Avoid relying on a single company, sector, token or market outcome. Diversification does not guarantee a profit or prevent losses.
  • Position sizing: Consider how much a loss on one position would affect your overall financial situation.
  • Liquidity awareness: Do not assume you can sell immediately at the last quoted price, especially in thinly traded penny stocks or tokens.
  • Leverage caution: Borrowing to invest or trading with leverage can magnify losses and may cause losses beyond the initial amount committed.
  • Scam awareness: Be skeptical of guaranteed returns, pressure to act immediately, secret signals, unsolicited messages and requests to transfer assets.
  • Independent verification: Confirm important information using official filings, regulator notices and primary project documentation.
  • Emotional discipline: Avoid chasing sharp price moves or making decisions to recover a previous loss.
Never invest money you cannot afford to lose in a highly speculative asset. This is especially important when researching thinly traded penny stocks, newly launched tokens and assets promoted mainly through social media.
Section 9

Stock market, penny stock and crypto glossary

These common investing terms appear in financial news, stock screeners, broker platforms and cryptocurrency research.

Ask price

The lowest displayed price a seller is currently willing to accept.

Bid price

The highest displayed price a buyer is currently willing to pay.

Bid-ask spread

The difference between the bid and ask prices.

Market cap

For a company, share price multiplied by shares outstanding. For crypto, commonly token price multiplied by circulating supply.

Liquidity

How easily an asset can be bought or sold without significantly moving its price.

Volatility

The degree to which an asset's price fluctuates over time.

Dividend

A distribution a company may pay to shareholders. It is not guaranteed.

Dilution

A reduction in existing shareholders' ownership percentage when additional shares are issued.

ETF

An exchange-traded fund that holds a basket of assets or tracks an index or strategy.

Fundamental analysis

Evaluating an asset using business, financial, economic or project information.

Technical analysis

Studying price, volume and chart-based measures.

Tokenomics

The design of a crypto token's supply, distribution, incentives and use.

Section 10

Frequently asked questions about stocks, penny stocks and crypto

What is the difference between stocks and cryptocurrency?

A stock generally represents an ownership interest in a company. A cryptocurrency is a digital asset whose features depend on its protocol and design. Holding a token does not normally give you shareholder rights in a company.

What is considered a penny stock?

The definition varies. In the United States, penny stocks are commonly associated with shares below US$5, but price alone does not determine every regulatory classification. Canadian usage and exchange rules can differ.

Are penny stocks good investments?

Some small companies may grow, but penny stocks can have substantial risks, including low liquidity, wide spreads, limited information, dilution and manipulation. Research the issuer and trading venue carefully; a low share price is not proof of value.

How do I find penny stocks with high volume?

A stock screener can filter for trading volume and average volume. Then verify the company's filings, news, share structure, liquidity and bid-ask spread. Unusual volume alone does not establish that a stock is a sound investment.

How do beginners start investing in stocks?

Learn basic market concepts, set financial goals, understand fees and account types, research diversified investment options and consider speaking with a qualified financial professional. Do not begin with money needed for essential expenses.

How do I evaluate a stock before buying it?

Understand the business, review revenue and earnings, cash flow, debt, management, competition and valuation. Read current company filings and consider risks, liquidity and your own time horizon.

Is cryptocurrency riskier than stocks?

Risk varies widely. Many cryptocurrencies have high volatility and additional technology, custody, platform and regulatory risks. Individual stocks also range from relatively established businesses to highly speculative companies. Compare the specific asset and risks rather than assuming every asset in a category is alike.

Can a stock with a low price be expensive?

Yes. Share price alone is not a valuation measure. Consider the number of shares outstanding, market capitalization, debt, cash flow, earnings and the company's prospects.

What is the best stock to buy today?

There is no single best stock for every investor. A decision depends on goals, risk tolerance, time horizon, valuation and current information. Treat daily stock lists as research starting points, not personalized recommendations.

What does trading volume tell investors?

Volume indicates how many shares or units traded during a period. It can help assess activity and liquidity, but reported volume should be interpreted alongside spreads, market depth, venue quality and price movement.

What is the difference between investing and trading?

Investing often focuses on owning assets over a longer period based on expected value or income. Trading generally focuses on shorter-term price movements. The distinction is not absolute, and both involve risk.

Can I lose all my money in penny stocks or crypto?

Yes. A company can fail or its shares can become worthless, and a digital asset can lose most or all of its value. Platform failure, fraud, lost private keys or liquidity problems can create additional losses.
