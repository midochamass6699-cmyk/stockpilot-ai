Margin Account vs. Cash Account: How Borrowing to Invest Really Works

Could you control $10,000 worth of stock with $5,000 of your own money? Learn how leverage works, what borrowing costs, and how a market drop can turn an opportunity into a serious loss.

1. What Is a Margin Account?

A margin account is a brokerage account that may allow you to borrow money from your broker to buy eligible investments. Your cash and eligible securities help secure the loan.

Think of it as a loan attached to your investment account. The borrowed money increases your purchasing power, but it is still debt: you must repay the principal and interest whether your investments rise or fall.

The key word is leverage. Leverage means using borrowed money to control a larger investment than you could buy using only your own cash. It magnifies gains and losses.

You do not have to borrow simply because you open a margin account. You can keep borrowing at zero, but always check your broker's rules, currency balances and account fees.

2. Cash Account vs. Margin Account

A cash account generally requires you to pay for purchases with available funds. A margin account may allow borrowing against eligible collateral, subject to approval and margin requirements.

FeatureCash accountMargin account
Buy with your own available cashYesYes
Borrow to buy eligible securitiesNoPotentially, if approved
Interest on borrowed balanceNot a margin loan; negative balances may still incur chargesYes, on the debit balance
Margin maintenance requirementsNoYes
Margin call / broker liquidationNo margin callPossible
Short sellingGenerally not supportedMay be available, subject to approval
Typical useInvesting with money you haveInvesting with optional leverage

Exact features vary by brokerage and account agreement. A margin account is not automatically a better account; it simply offers additional borrowing and trading capabilities, with additional risks.

3. How Much Can You Borrow?

There is no universal borrowing limit for every investor or every stock. Your available margin depends on the value and type of collateral, the broker's loan value for each security, concentration limits, market conditions and regulatory requirements.

For a simplified illustration, imagine a broker allows a 50% initial margin requirement on a particular eligible stock. That means you provide half the purchase price and borrow the other half.

Important: 50% is an example, not a promise or a universal Canadian rule. A stock may require more equity, have reduced loan value, or be ineligible for margin. Your broker's live buying-power and margin figures control.

4. How Leverage Changes Your Profit—and Your Loss

Suppose you invest $5,000 of your own money and borrow another $5,000, creating a $10,000 position. Ignore interest, fees and taxes for this simplified example.

Stock rises 10%

+$1,000

$10,000 position becomes $11,000. After repaying the $5,000 loan, you have $6,000 in equity: a 20% gain on your original $5,000.

Stock falls 20%

−$2,000

$10,000 position becomes $8,000. After the $5,000 loan, you have $3,000 in equity: a 40% loss on your original $5,000.

The percentage move in the stock is not the same as the percentage move in your own equity when you use leverage. Interest and fees make the actual result less favourable.

What if the stock falls 50%?

The $10,000 position would be worth $5,000. If you still owe $5,000, your equity is zero before interest and costs. You may still owe money if losses and expenses exceed the value of your collateral.

Your equity = Current market value of holdings − Amount you owe

5. What Is a Margin Call?

A margin call or margin deficiency occurs when your account equity falls below the amount your broker requires you to maintain. This can happen because investments fall, the broker raises a security's margin requirement, or other account balances change.

Your broker may require you to deposit cash or eligible securities, reduce the loan, or close positions. Brokerage agreements can permit the firm to sell securities to address a deficiency, sometimes without advance notice and without letting you choose which holdings are sold.

Do not assume you will have several days to react. Read your broker's margin agreement and liquidation policy before borrowing. A fast market decline can leave little time to act.

6. Margin Interest Rates: What Does Borrowing Cost?

Margin interest is generally charged on the amount you borrow, based on the currency, loan size, broker pricing tier and applicable rate. Rates are variable and can change. Interest is commonly calculated using daily balances and posted periodically.

A real Canadian broker example

Qtrade's published pricing page listed a Canadian prime rate of 4.70% (October 29, 2026) and a U.S. prime rate of 7.00% (September 17, 2026). For margin debit balances below $100,000, its listed standard rate was prime plus 1.55%: an illustrative 6.25% CAD rate and 8.55% USD rate on those stated dates. Pricing can change, and account-specific terms may differ.

Before borrowing, check the current rate for your exact account and currency. Compare the borrowing cost with a realistic—not guaranteed—investment return. A stock can rise and still leave you with a poor net result after interest, fees and taxes.

7. Can You Borrow Against Stocks You Already Own?

Potentially. If you hold securities that your broker accepts as collateral, they may contribute to your account's borrowing power. But the broker determines each security's loan value, and that value can change.

If your holdings fall, become more volatile, lose liquidity or are removed from the eligible list, your borrowing power may shrink. A diversified portfolio does not eliminate margin risk, but concentration in one volatile stock can make it worse.

8. Can You Buy Penny Stocks on Margin?

Some low-priced securities may be ineligible for margin or may carry much higher margin requirements. Penny stocks can have thin trading volume, wide bid-ask spreads, limited public information and abrupt price moves.

Combining those characteristics with borrowed money can be especially dangerous. A sharp decline or lack of buyers can make it difficult to exit a position before a margin deficiency develops. Never assume a stock is marginable just because it appears in your trading app.

9. Can You Use Margin in a TFSA or RRSP?

Do not assume a registered account offers the same margin borrowing features as a non-registered margin account. Canadian registered plans have specific rules, and borrowing, short selling and other leveraged activity may be restricted or unavailable depending on the account and broker.

Ask your brokerage about the exact account type and applicable rules before moving money or placing an order. Do not try to create leverage inside a registered account without understanding the tax and regulatory consequences.

10. Are Margin Interest Payments Tax-Deductible in Canada?

Interest on money borrowed to earn income may be deductible in some circumstances under Canadian tax rules. Eligibility depends on the purpose and use of the borrowed funds, the type of investment and other conditions. It is not automatically deductible simply because the loan is in a margin account.

Keep records showing where borrowed funds went, avoid mixing personal and investment borrowing, and consult a qualified Canadian tax professional about your circumstances before claiming a deduction.

11. Who Should Consider a Margin Account?

A margin account may be appropriate for some experienced investors who understand leverage, have stable finances, can withstand losses, maintain liquidity and have a clear plan to repay borrowing. Even then, using the maximum available margin is not necessarily prudent.

It may be unsuitable for people who are new to investing, have little emergency savings, carry expensive debt, need the invested money soon, depend on investment gains to pay bills, or would be unable to meet a margin call.

Canadian securities regulator CIRO cautions that borrowing to invest increases risk: you remain responsible for the loan and interest even if the investment loses value, and losses can be substantially greater than with an unleveraged investment.

12. A Practical Checklist Before You Borrow

  • Confirm the security is eligible for margin and check its current requirement.
  • Find the exact interest rate for the currency and loan size.
  • Calculate the interest cost if the investment takes longer than expected.
  • Stress-test a 10%, 20% or larger decline in the portfolio.
  • Keep cash available to meet a margin deficiency without selling at a bad time.
  • Understand when and how the broker can liquidate positions.
  • Avoid relying on dividends or price appreciation to make loan payments.
  • Have a realistic repayment plan independent of the stock rising.
Bottom line: A margin account can increase buying power, but it does not increase your financial safety net. Borrowing magnifies both outcomes. Understand the downside first, and remember that choosing not to borrow is always an option.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I lose more than I deposit?

Yes. Depending on the position, market movement, interest and fees, you may owe more than the cash you initially contributed.

Do I pay margin interest if I do not borrow?

Generally, margin-loan interest applies when you have an outstanding debit balance. Other fees or negative balances may still generate charges, so check your broker's fee schedule.

Can I hold a margin position for years?

Brokerage rules may allow it, but interest generally continues while the debit balance remains. Long holding periods can make borrowing costly.

Can my broker sell my investments?

Yes. Your margin agreement may permit the broker to liquidate securities to address a shortfall. The timing and process depend on the agreement and applicable rules.

Is a margin account the same as a line of credit?

Both involve borrowing, but a margin loan is secured by eligible investments and is subject to changing collateral values and maintenance requirements.

Does a margin account mean I must borrow?

No. You can have margin privileges and choose not to carry a borrowed balance. Monitor cash and currency balances to avoid accidental borrowing.
