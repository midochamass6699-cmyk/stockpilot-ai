What this teaches

A call gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying shares at the strike price. A put gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to sell at the strike price. This simplified exercise model shows the option buyer's result at expiration, excluding fees, taxes and early exercise considerations.

The premium is paid upfront. The maximum loss for this long-option example is the premium paid. The result shown is a hypothetical educational calculation, not a forecast or recommendation.

Worked example: why break-even matters

Suppose a hypothetical call has a $55 strike and costs $2.50 per share. A standard equity option contract commonly represents 100 shares, so the initial premium for one contract is $250 before fees. At expiration, the simplified break-even price is $57.50: the $55 strike plus the $2.50 premium.

Expiration at $60$250 gain

Intrinsic value is $5 per share. The $500 option value minus the $250 premium produces a hypothetical $250 result.

The call expires out of the money in this simplified example. The buyer loses the $250 premium paid.

The same option can have a different result at an earlier date because an option's market price can include time value and implied volatility. This simulator deliberately does not model those factors.

What this simulator leaves out

This is an expiration-only intrinsic-value calculator for a long call or long put. It does not estimate an option's fair market price before expiration and does not model implied volatility, time decay, interest rates, dividends, bid–ask spreads, commissions, taxes, assignment, early exercise or changing contract specifications.

The default contract multiplier of 100 is a teaching assumption for a standard equity option. Adjusted contracts and some other option products can use different deliverables. Real contracts have expiration dates and standardized terms; the fictional inputs here are not quotes or tradable contracts.

Use the exercise to understand how strike, premium and expiration price interact—not to forecast an outcome or decide whether to trade.

How to interpret the simulator responsibly

The result is a simplified expiration payoff based on the inputs entered. It is not a live option valuation. The calculation does not estimate implied volatility, time value before expiration, interest rates, dividends, early exercise, bid–ask spread, commissions, taxes or changing contract deliverables.

Use the tool to investigate these questions

  • How does changing the strike affect expiration break-even?
  • How does the premium change the amount that must be recovered?
  • What happens if the underlying finishes on the other side of the strike?
  • How does contract count scale both the potential result and amount at risk?

Compare the simulator with actual contract specifications and official disclosure documents. A simplified model teaches mechanics; it does not predict market outcomes.
